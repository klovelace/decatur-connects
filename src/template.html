Citizen proposal · City of Decatur, Georgia · November 2026

Decatur Connects

A protected bike network that links all nine public schools, the Square, three MARTA stations and Oakhurst Village, with a new north–south connector on the west side. It builds on the city's adopted Safe Streets Safety Action Plan and the projects already under way. Lanes come out only where traffic volumes allow it. The busiest roads get paths beside the street instead.

Prepared from the city's 2019–2023 crash records, the 2025 Safety Action Plan, the 2018 Community Transportation Plan, 2026 project materials, and OpenStreetMap street data.

What the crash records show

Decatur recorded 3,451 crashes from 2019 to 2023. People walking and biking were in only 3% of them but made up 40% of fatal and serious-injury crashes. The figures below come from the city's own crash layer, the same data behind the Safety Action Plan, filtered to the 38 bicycle and 55 pedestrian crashes.

What this means for design. Most bike crashes happen where there is no bike facility and a driver is turning. A painted line does not stop a turning driver. Protected intersections and hardened turn lines do, so every corridor below includes its intersections, not only the blocks between them. The two 2023 right-hook crashes on the Church Street cycle track show that protection fails where it is interrupted by driveways. Those gaps need fixing too.

Design rules: what the evidence supports

Yes, parking cars on the outside is a recognized best practice. FHWA's Separated Bike Lane Planning and Design Guide and NACTO's Urban Bikeway Design Guide both list on-street parking as a form of vertical separation. Swapping the parking lane and the bike lane, so parked cars sit between moving traffic and people on bikes, gives protection at almost no cost and keeps the parking. It works only with three details: a 3-ft buffer so opening car doors do not hit riders, daylighting (no parking 20–30 ft before crosswalks and driveways) so drivers and riders can see each other, and flex posts in the buffer so the lane stays protected when the spaces are empty.

7

Keeping traffic moving

No lanes removed on the busiest roads

Scott Blvd carries about 40,000 vehicles a day and keeps all four lanes. Riders get a separate path behind the curb. E College Ave and E Ponce de Leon Ave, at 23,000–28,000 a day, go to GDOT and city corridor studies before any lane changes.

Road diets only where volumes fit

The 4-to-3 conversions are on Clairemont (19,300), Commerce (12,500–13,000) and downtown Church St. All are within the range where FHWA finds a center turn lane offsets the lost through lane, because left-turning cars no longer block the inside lane.

Parking kept, not removed

Parking-protected layouts on W Ponce and Church St keep the parking or add to it; the Church St option adds 9–11 spaces. Removing parking is a last resort, used only at daylighted corners.

Build cheap first, then measure

Phase 1 uses paint, posts and planters, as Decatur did on W Howard in 2018 and plans for Church St. Travel times, speeds and bike counts get measured for 6–12 months before concrete is poured, so any problem can be fixed for little cost.

8

Phasing and funding

Funding paths. Adopting the Safety Action Plan made Decatur eligible for SS4A Implementation grants, which fit the HIN corridors (Clairemont, Commerce, E College). Scott Blvd already has a $1.87M RAISE planning grant. State routes (Clairemont, Commerce, College, Scott) can go through GDOT Quick Response and safety programs. Greenways and quick-builds fit ARC Transportation Alternatives and the city's capital and SPLOST budget. The S McDonough–Oakview spine is already in the adopted PATH plan.

Method and limits. Corridors were snapped to OpenStreetMap streets and measured along the street. A crash counts as "on the network" if it lies within 40 m (130 ft) of a proposed route. School access is measured as the straight-line distance from each school to the nearest protected bikeway, trail or greenway. OSM may not show every existing facility, such as the newest W Howard cycle track. Traffic volumes other than Clairemont and Commerce are planning estimates. This is a citizen proposal for discussion, not an engineering design. Every corridor needs survey, traffic analysis, and GDOT review where it is a state route.
